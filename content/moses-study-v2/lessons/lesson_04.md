# Lesson 4 — The Excuses

**life_stage:** Calling
**key_passage:** Exodus 4:1-17
**character_qualities:** null

## story_narrative

The bush is still burning. God has seen, heard, known, promised, named himself, and given Moses the entire plan including how it ends.

And Moses says: *But they will not believe me.*

What follows is one of the most uncomfortable conversations in Scripture, because it is not a man wrestling with a hard calling — it is a man negotiating his way out of one. Four objections, each answered, each replaced by another. *They won't believe me.* God gives him three signs. *I'm not a good speaker.* God points out who made mouths in the first place and promises to supply the words. And when every argument has been dismantled, what is underneath them finally comes out, stripped of all its reasoning: *O my Lord, send somebody else.*

That is when the text says the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses.

But watch what God's anger actually does. He does not withdraw the call. He does not find another man. He says: *Is not Aaron your brother? He is already on his way to meet you, and when he sees you he will be glad.* The rebuke and the provision arrive in the same breath, and the provision was in motion before Moses ever made the request.

And at the center of the whole exchange sits the simplest question God asks anyone in the Old Testament, the one that dismantles forty years of accumulated inadequacy in five words.

*What is that in your hand?*

A stick. A shepherd's staff. The thing he had leaned on every day for four decades of obscurity, so ordinary he had stopped seeing it.

God did not give Moses new credentials. He told him to throw down the one he already had, pick it back up, and go.

## verse_sections

### [1-9] What Is That in Your Hand

Moses's first objection sounds reasonable and is quietly ironic. *They will not believe me* — and the Hebrew for believe is *aman* (H539), the root behind *amen*. It is not a word about mental agreement. *Aman* is a builder's word: to make firm, to prop up, to establish something so it holds weight. It is used of a nursing mother holding a child steady. To *aman* someone is to lean your full weight on them the way you trust a beam to hold the ceiling.

So Moses is predicting that no one will rest their weight on his word — while he is standing in front of a burning bush declining to rest his weight on God's. He is diagnosing in others the exact condition he is currently in.

And God's response is not an argument. It is a question about an object. *What is that in thine hand?*

*A rod.* The Hebrew is *matteh* (H4294), and English loses almost everything. A *matteh* is a branch cut from a living tree — a humble thing, a walking stick. But the same word means a tribal scepter, the emblem of authority a leader carries. One word holding humble origin, daily dependence, and ruling power at once.

Moses had been leaning on a scepter for forty years and calling it a stick. That is the whole answer to his inadequacy, and God delivers it without a word of explanation. He does not hand Moses impressive new equipment. He reframes what Moses has been carrying through the entire wasted season.

Then: throw it down. And it becomes a serpent, and Moses *fled* — *nus* (H5127), the verb for running from an advancing army or a flash flood. Not a careful step back. A full, undignified sprint from something that had been a walking stick in his own hand a second earlier.

That detail is worth sitting with, because it is honest about what surrender costs. The thing God transforms in your hands may terrify you before it frees anyone else.

And God says: pick it up by the tail — which is, of course, the wrong end, the end that leaves the head free. The word for what Moses does is *chazaq* (H2388), and it is not casual. It means to fasten onto with strength, to seize and hold firm, to fortify yourself for an action. It is the word used for courage in battle and for repairing broken walls. Moses did not gingerly retrieve his staff. He gripped it — a deliberate act of strength at the precise moment fear was loudest.

The second sign moves inward. God tells him to put his hand into his *bosom*, *cheiq* (H2436) — not just the chest but the fold of a garment held against the body, the most protected place a person had, where you would shelter a child or cradle something precious. His hand comes out ruined. Back in, and it comes out restored *as his other flesh* — *basar* (H1320), the word for warm, living, fully-alive body, the same word used when God forms Adam. Not patched. Not scarred. Returned whole.

God works the miracle through the most intimate space Moses owns, not at arm's length. Both the ruin and the healing originate in the hidden place where no one else can see.

And then a sobering note in the middle of the provision. God says: if they will not *hearken* to the first sign, they will believe the second. *Shama* (H8085) never means bare hearing; Hebrew draws no clean line between hearing and responding. To *shama* is to receive something so completely it changes what you do. So God is describing a specific kind of hardness — people who watch the impossible happen and still keep the door locked. He knows some of them will. And he sends a second sign anyway, and then a third.

### [10-12] A Heavy Tongue Before the God Who Makes Mouths

Moses tries again, and this time it is not about the people. It is about himself. *I am not eloquent... I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue.*

The word is *kabed* (H3515), and it means *heavy*. It is the root for something burdensome, laden, hard to move. Moses is not describing an occasional stumble. He is describing a mouth that will not lift, a tongue weighted down like a load too heavy to carry.

And here the enrichment surfaces something that reorganizes the entire book of Exodus: ***kabed* is the same word used, over and over, for Pharaoh's hardened heart.** Pharaoh's heart is *heavied*. Moses's tongue is *heavy*. The man is standing there explaining that he is too weighted to be used — and God is about to send that exact weight straight at a heavier one, and win.

What you are certain disqualifies you may be the precise territory God intends to work in.

God's answer is a question that ends the argument. *Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb?* The word for dumb is *'illem* (H483), and it means literally *bound* or *sealed* — picture a jar with the lid pressed tight. That is the image behind a mouth that cannot speak: something closed.

And the implication lands like a stone. If God is the one who seals a mouth, God is the one who can open it. Moses is a closed jar standing in front of the God who opens and shuts every human vessel, explaining to the Potter what the clay is not able to do.

Then comes the promise, and the verb is astonishing. *I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt say.* Teach is *yarah* (H3384) — to shoot an arrow, to throw water with aim. It is the root behind *Torah*. God is not offering Moses a speech course or a period of preparation. He is promising live, targeted supply: the right words fired into the moment, arriving exactly when the mouth opens.

That is a completely different kind of confidence from self-assurance. Moses does not have to generate anything in advance. He has to open his mouth on time.

### [13-17] "Send Somebody Else"

Every objection has now been answered. And Moses says: *O my Lord, send, I pray thee, by the hand of him whom thou wilt send.*

The word is *yad* (H3027) — hand, but in Hebrew thought the hand is power, agency, authority, a person's will extended into the world. Moses is not asking God to send another body. He is saying: *give the whole thing — the power, the authority, the commission — to someone else.* He is handing back the hand before he has even closed his fingers around it.

And the text says plainly: *the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses.*

It is important not to soften that. Four chapters of divine patience have a limit, and Moses found it. This last request was not humility; it was refusal wearing humility's clothes. The signs had been given. The name had been given. The promise of supplied words had been given. There was nothing left to answer, and Moses asked anyway.

But look at what God's anger produces. *Is not Aaron the Levite thy brother? I know that he can speak well. And also, behold, he cometh forth to meet thee.*

Aaron is *already walking.* He was dispatched before Moses made the request. God's anger and God's provision arrive in the same sentence, and the provision predates the refusal that prompted it.

And the detail God chooses to add is not strategic, it is tender: *when he seeth thee, he will be glad in his heart.* The heart is *lev* (H3820) — not the organ but the core of a person, the seat of will and thought and deep feeling. This is not a polite greeting at the door. God is telling a man who feels alone and unqualified that something is going to ignite at the very center of his brother when he lays eyes on him. God's answer to Moses's isolation was not a pep talk. It was a brother on the road with joy already rising in his chest.

Then the arrangement: Aaron will be *instead of a mouth* to you. The word is *peh* (H6310), and it is more concrete than English suggests — *peh* is the edge of a sword, the opening of a cave, the rim of a vessel. It is a threshold word: the place where what is inside gets out.

That reframes everything. Moses is not being replaced. He has the substance; he lacks an exit. Aaron is the opening. Your limitation may not be a disqualification — it may simply mean God is building you a *peh*.

And the chapter's final word on the subject returns to where it started: *thou shalt take this rod in thine hand.* The *matteh*. The same stick. He is not sent out with anything he did not already have.

## key_insights

1. God's first answer to inadequacy was a question about an object already in Moses's hand. The *matteh* was a scepter the whole time — God reframed forty years of obscurity rather than replacing it with credentials.
2. Moses predicted the people would not *aman* him while standing inside that exact unbelief himself. We are often most fluent in diagnosing the condition we are currently in.
3. *Kabed* is the hinge of the book: Moses's heavy tongue and Pharaoh's heavy heart are the same word. The thing Moses named as his disqualification was precisely the territory God chose to work in.
4. *'Illem* means sealed, like a lidded jar — and the God who seals a mouth can open one. Moses was explaining to the Potter what the clay could not do.
5. *Yarah* is an archery word. God did not promise Moses eloquence or preparation; he promised the right words shot into the moment, arriving as the mouth opened.
6. God's anger and God's provision came in the same breath, and Aaron was already walking. The help was dispatched before the refusal that seemed to prompt it.

## hard_truths

1. God does get angry with reluctance. Four objections were met with patience; the fifth — *send someone else* — was met with kindled anger, and Scripture does not apologize for it.
2. There is a point at which "I'm not qualified" stops being humility and becomes disobedience. The line is crossed when every objection has been answered and you ask anyway.
3. Moses got Aaron, and it cost him something. A mouthpiece was provision, not upgrade — and the brother who spoke for him here is the same brother who will build the golden calf.
4. The thing God transforms in your hands will likely frighten you first. Moses *nus*'d — ran outright — from his own staff.
5. God told Moses in advance that some people would see a miracle and still not *shama*. Signs do not guarantee reception, and obedience does not come with a promise of being believed.
6. Nothing new was given. Moses walked back into Egypt with the same stick, the same tongue, and the same history — only the presence of God had changed.

## about_god

He answers objections with provision rather than rebuke, right up until the moment the objection is no longer honest. He works from the inside out — the hidden *cheiq*, the sealed mouth — rather than at arm's length. He claims responsibility for human limitation itself, which is a hard and enormous thing to say, and then uses it. And his help is usually already in motion before we ask; Aaron was on the road while Moses was still arguing.

## about_ourselves

We will keep generating objections as long as the objections are working, and the real one is usually the last one. Underneath our reasoning about competence is very often simple unwillingness, and it can sound remarkably pious. We also chronically fail to see what is already in our hands, because familiarity makes a scepter look like a stick. And we tend to want to be capable more than we want to be sent.

## failures_struggles

This is Moses's failure, and it is sustained across an entire chapter. Four objections deep, with every one answered by direct divine provision, he asks God to send someone else — and Scripture calls the response to that anger, not sympathy. The root of it is not modesty. It is that Moses would rather stay in Midian than risk Egypt again, and he has enough spiritual vocabulary to make that sound like self-awareness. There is also something unresolved in him from forty years earlier: the man who once acted violently on his own authority now will not act on God's. Overreach curdled into paralysis, and both are failures to let God set the terms. He does eventually go. But he goes rebuked, with a brother assigned to cover the part of himself he would not surrender.

## specific_applications

1. **If you are waiting to be equipped before you obey** — Answer God's question: what is already in your hand? The forty years you consider wasted almost certainly put something there you have stopped seeing.
2. **If you keep producing reasons you are not the right person** — Notice how many objections deep you are. The first is usually sincere; by the fourth you are negotiating, and the honest version is probably *I don't want to go.*
3. **If a specific weakness feels like your disqualification** — Moses's heavy tongue was aimed at a heavy heart. Name the weakness plainly to God rather than using it as an exit, and watch whether it turns out to be the assignment's actual terrain.
4. **If you feel alone in something God has asked of you** — Aaron was already walking before Moses asked. Look for the help that is likely already en route rather than assuming you are unaccompanied.
5. **If surrendering something has frightened you** — Moses ran from his own staff on the ground. Fear at the moment of release is not a sign you heard wrong; *chazaq* — grip it — is what comes next.

## reflection_questions

1. What is in your hand right now that you have written off as ordinary, and what would change if you saw it as the thing God intends to use?
2. Which of your objections is the honest one, and which are the ones you deploy because they work?
3. What limitation are you most certain disqualifies you — and have you ever considered that it might be the exact ground God means to work on?
4. Where have you asked God to send someone else, in words polite enough that you did not have to hear yourself do it?
5. What has God transformed in your hands that frightened you before it helped anyone, and did you pick it back up?
6. Who is already walking toward you — help you have not yet recognized or accepted because you assumed you were on your own?
7. Where have you seen something undeniable and still not let it land, not *shama*'d it into any actual change?
