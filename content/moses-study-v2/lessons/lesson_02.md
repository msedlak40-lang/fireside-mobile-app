# Lesson 2 — Murder and Flight

**life_stage:** Exile
**key_passage:** Exodus 2:11-25
**character_qualities:** null

## story_narrative

Forty years pass in a single verse. The baby in the basket is now a grown man in Pharaoh's house, and one day he walks out of it.

That walk is the whole hinge. Moses could have stayed insulated — the palace had every reason to keep him comfortable and every resource to do it. Instead he goes out to where his people are working, and the text says he *looked on their burdens*. Not glanced. Looked. He made himself see what the empire had spent his entire childhood making invisible to him.

And then he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, and something in him broke loose. He looked this way and that way. He saw no one. He killed the man and buried him in the sand.

The next day he went out again and found two Hebrews fighting each other, and when he stepped in, the man throwing punches turned the question back on him: *Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you intend to kill me like you killed the Egyptian?* Moses had imagined himself a deliverer. What he got was a witness and a threat. Pharaoh heard. Moses ran.

He ran east until Egypt was behind him, and then he sat down by a well in Midian — a man with no country, no people, and no plan, forty years of privilege gone in two days.

And at that well he did the same thing that had cost him everything, except this time he did it right. Shepherds were bullying seven women away from the water. Moses stood up, drove them off, and then stayed and watered their flock. Nobody asked. He had nothing to gain. He was nobody there. He did it anyway. That small act of justice bought him a meal, then a home, then a wife, then a son — and he named the boy *Gershom*, a stranger here, because that is what he had become and he was not going to pretend otherwise.

Then the chapter turns away from Moses entirely. Back in Egypt the king died, the slavery ground on, and the people finally screamed. And the last three verses say four things in a row: God heard. God remembered. God looked. God knew.

Moses thought he had blown it. God was already moving.

## verse_sections

### [11-15] The Prince Who Looked Too Closely

Everything starts with a word most readers skip. Moses *looked on their burdens*, and the Hebrew for burdens is *siblotam* (H5450), from a root meaning porterage — the hauling of loads too heavy for a body to carry. It is not an abstraction. It is the picture of a person bent double under something they can neither put down nor escape. And Moses did not glance at it. He fixed his eyes on the weight pressing his people into the ground.

Hold onto that, because it is the one thing Moses does right in this passage. Seeing suffering clearly — refusing to soften it, explain it, or look past it — is where God's work in a person usually starts. Moses could not free anyone that day. He could only refuse to stop looking.

Then he kills a man, and the text gives us a second verb worth slowing down for. He *hid* the body — *taman* (H2934), to conceal by burying, by covering something over. It is the word for hiding treasure or setting a trap. Picture him on his knees in the sand, head down, working fast, scooping dirt over a corpse. That image tells you everything about his own verdict on what he just did. This is not a man at peace with an act of justice. This is a man covering his tracks. He knew the difference between justice and secrecy, and he chose secrecy.

It lasted about a day. When the Hebrew throws the killing back in his face, Moses says, *surely this thing is known* — and the Hebrew is *noda*, from *yada* (H3045), which is never merely "the facts got out." *Yada* is deep knowing: God knowing a person, truth being recognized rather than just discovered. Moses is not saying the secret leaked. He is saying it has *landed*. What he buried has surfaced, and there is no putting it back in the sand.

So he runs, and the verb is *barach* (H1272) — to bolt, the sudden flight of a startled animal. There is panic in it. This is not a strategic withdrawal; it is a sprint. And then the verse does something remarkable: the man who bolted *sits down* by a well. From *barach* to stillness in a single line. The wilderness looked like the end of him. It was the classroom.

### [16-22] A Well in Midian

Seven women come to water their father's flock, and the shepherds shove them aside. Moses — a fugitive with no standing, no name, and every reason to keep his head down — stands up.

The word for what he does is *yasha* (H3467), and it is not a polite word for lending a hand. Its root picture is a constricted space suddenly thrown open, a narrowness made wide. It is the word the Old Testament uses for salvation, for rescue, for deliverance. Scripture reaches for the vocabulary of the Exodus to describe a nobody breaking up a scuffle at a watering trough, forty years early.

That is worth sitting with. The instinct that got him exiled in Egypt is the same instinct he acts on here. What changed is not the impulse but the method — no secrecy, no killing, no glancing around first to see who is watching. He simply steps in, in daylight, and then does the unglamorous part: he stays and waters the flock. The deliverer is being trained, and the training is happening in obscurity, on a scale nobody would call significant.

The daughters report it to their father, and the word they choose is *natsal* (H5337) — to snatch, to yank something away from danger the way you grab a child before they step into traffic. Not "he helped us." *He tore us out of their hands.* The same verb God will later use for what he does to Israel.

Reuel's response is one word of hospitality with a lot of weight in it: bring him in, let him eat *lechem* (H3899). Bread in Hebrew is not a convenience item. *Lechem* is grain ground, mixed, and fired — the costly, labored-over thing that keeps a person alive. Reuel is not offering a snack. He is saying a man who defends the defenseless gets the full table, not scraps.

And Moses stays. The text says he *was content*, which renders *ya'el* (H2974) — to yield, to willingly undertake, to say yes with your whole will. It is not resignation. Moses did not slump into Midian defeated; he made an inward decision to let this be his life. Then he names his son *Gershom*, and the root is *ger* (H1616) — not a tourist, but a resident alien: living permanently among people not your own, with no land claim, no legal protection, dependent on someone else's goodwill. Moses pressed his entire autobiography into his son's name. *I have no standing here.* He said the true thing out loud, and then kept building a life inside it.

### [23-25] The God Who Was Never Elsewhere

The camera cuts away from Midian, and for three verses Moses does not appear at all.

In Egypt the king dies, and nothing improves. The slavery grinds on under the next administration, which is its own kind of cruelty — the hope of a regime change, and then the same whip. And somewhere in that, the people stop merely enduring and start screaming. The word is *zaaq* (H2199), and it is not a prayer. It is a public shriek, the sound a herald makes to raise a town, the noise torn out of a body in genuine danger. It is loud, undignified, and unpolished.

That is what reached God. The text does not say they prayed correctly. It says they *zaaq*'d, and it went up.

Then verse 24 doubles down with an even rawer word: God heard their *groaning*, *na'aqah* (H5009) — the sound a person makes when pain has bypassed language altogether. Not a petition. Not even sentences. Just the noise of a body under a weight too heavy to describe. God did not wait for them to format the request. The groan itself was sufficient.

And then four verbs land in a row, and they are the turning point of the entire book. God *heard*. God *remembered* his covenant — which in Hebrew never means he had forgotten, but that he is now *acting* on what was always true. God *looked* — *ra'ah* (H7200), and in the ancient world sight implied intention; when a king looked upon someone he was deciding their fate. It is the same verb used when God sees Hagar in the desert and when he provides the ram for Abraham. It is a seeing that moves toward action.

And God *knew*.

Notice where Moses is while all of this happens. He is a shepherd in a foreign country, forty years into what he almost certainly regards as the wreckage of his one attempt to help. He has no idea any of this is occurring. The chapter ends with God fully mobilized and the deliverer entirely unaware — which means the long silence Moses was living inside was never God's absence. It was the interval between God seeing and God sending.

## key_insights

1. Moses's first right instinct and his worst sin are the same impulse one verse apart. He *looked on their burdens* — and then he killed a man. The problem was never that he cared; it was that he acted on it without God, without authority, and in secret.
2. *Yasha* at the well is the tell. The same protective instinct that destroyed his life in Egypt becomes the language of salvation in Midian, because he finally acted in daylight and then stayed to do the unglamorous part.
3. What we bury surfaces. Moses *taman*'d a body in the sand and within a day the thing was *yada* — not merely discovered, but landed, recognized, irreversible.
4. Exile was not punishment; it was curriculum. Forty years of shepherding another man's flock in a country where he had no standing is exactly the resume required to lead a stubborn people through a wilderness.
5. The chapter's last three verses prove God was already moving while Moses assumed the story was over. *Zaaq*, *na'aqah*, *ra'ah* — the rescue was fully underway and its appointed deliverer had no idea.

## hard_truths

1. Good intentions do not sanctify the method. Moses was right about the injustice and wrong about everything he did next, and being right about the problem did not spare him a single consequence.
2. Looking around to see if anyone is watching is a confession. If you have to check first, you already know.
3. The people you are trying to help may be the ones who expose you — and they may be right to. Moses was not appointed. He appointed himself, and a fellow Hebrew called it exactly what it was.
4. Forty years is a long time to be wrong about what God is doing. Moses spent four decades believing he had forfeited his purpose, and the text gives no indication he was ever told otherwise during them.
5. God heard the cry — and the slavery still continued while he acted. Being seen by God is real, and it does not mean the weight lifts this week.
6. Naming yourself honestly costs something. Moses called his son *a stranger here* rather than pretending Midian was home, and living in that truth is lonelier than living in a comfortable fiction.

## about_god

God hears what is not addressed to him and answers what cannot form itself into words. The *zaaq* was a scream, the *na'aqah* was barely human noise, and both reached him intact. He remembers covenants across generations of apparent silence — not recalling what he forgot, but acting on what was never in question. And his *ra'ah* is not observation from a distance; it is the fixed attention of someone who has decided to intervene. He was watching the labor camps the entire time Moses was watching sheep.

## about_ourselves

We are quick to mistake our passion for our commission. Moses had genuine compassion, real courage, and no mandate, and the gap between those cost him forty years. We also tend to read our consequences as disqualification — assuming that because we blew it, God's purposes for us closed. And we are almost always working with a smaller picture than the one we are inside of. Moses had no way of knowing that his worst season and God's decisive movement were happening simultaneously.

## failures_struggles

This is Moses's own failure, and it is not a small one. He committed murder, concealed it, and told himself the sand would hold. Underneath the act is the pattern that will shadow him for the rest of his life: he sees something wrong and reaches for force before he reaches for God. He also assumed an authority he had not been given — the Hebrew's "who made you a prince and a judge over us?" is an ugly question and a fair one. And when it came apart, his response was not repentance but flight. He ran to Midian, sat down by a well, and stayed forty years. The wilderness did its work on him, but he did not choose it; he bolted into it.

## specific_applications

1. **If you are carrying anger at a real injustice** — Moses was not wrong to be furious. He was wrong to act alone, in secret, and by force. Take the seeing to God before you take the action anywhere.
2. **If you are hiding something** — Sand is not a strategy. What Moses buried surfaced within a day, and the exposure was worse than the confession would have been. Bring it into the light while the choice is still yours.
3. **If your past has convinced you God is done with you** — The last three verses of this chapter happen while Moses is herding sheep with no idea. Your disqualifying season and God's preparation are frequently the same season.
4. **If you are in an obscure stretch where nothing you do seems to matter** — The well in Midian is where Moses practiced *yasha* with no audience. Faithfulness in a place nobody is watching is how the instinct gets trained for the place everyone will be.
5. **If your pain has outrun your words** — *Na'aqah* is the groan with no sentences in it, and it is the one God explicitly heard. You do not owe God a well-formed prayer before he will take your suffering seriously.

## reflection_questions

1. Whose burden have you been aware of but never actually stopped and *looked* at — and what would looking properly cost you?
2. Where are you tempted to fix something by force because waiting on God feels like doing nothing?
3. What have you buried and covered over, hoping the sand will hold — and what would it take to bring it up yourself before it surfaces on its own?
4. Have you appointed yourself to something God has not actually given you authority over?
5. What season of your life are you writing off as wasted, and what might be getting built in you there that could not be built anywhere else?
6. Is there a place where you are being faithful with no audience, and have you concluded that means it does not count?
7. What have you been silently enduring that you have not yet let yourself cry out to God about?
