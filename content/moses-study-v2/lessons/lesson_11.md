# Lesson 11 — The Ten Commandments

**life_stage:** Covenant
**key_passage:** Exodus 20:1-26
**character_qualities:** null

## story_narrative

Three months out of Egypt, they come to a mountain, and the mountain is on fire.

Thunder. A trumpet blast that keeps getting louder. Smoke going up like a furnace. And then, for the only time in Scripture, God speaks an entire law code aloud to an entire nation at once — no prophet in between, no intermediary, the whole people standing at the base of the mountain hearing the voice directly.

And the first thing he says is not a command. It is a history.

*I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.*

Before a single requirement, God establishes who is speaking and what he has already done. This is not a stranger issuing terms to strangers. It is the one who already broke the chains, speaking to people who are standing there free because of it.

Then ten sentences. No other gods. No carved images. Do not carry my name hollow. Keep the seventh day set apart. Give your parents weight. Do not shatter a life. Do not betray a covenant. Do not take by stealth. Do not build a false structure around someone's name. And do not sit with your neighbor's life in your imagination until you have made it yours.

The people cannot bear it. They back away from the mountain and beg Moses: *you speak to us, and we will listen — but do not let God speak to us, or we will die.*

And Moses gives them an answer that sounds like a contradiction and is not: *Fear not — for God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces, that ye sin not.*

Then the people stand at a distance, and Moses walks into the thick darkness where God is.

## verse_sections

### [1-6] A Rescuer, Not a Stranger

The whole architecture of the Ten Commandments rests on the sentence that comes before them.

*I brought you out of the house of bondage.* The word is *bayit* (H1004), and it means far more than a building. A *bayit* is a household, a social structure, an entire organized world. So "house of bondage" is not describing Egypt as a hard workplace. It is describing a complete social reality in which these people's identity was *property*.

God is not claiming to have relocated them. He is claiming to have extracted them from a system built to own them.

And that claim is the foundation everything else stands on. The law is not the price of belonging to God. It is the portrait of what belonging to him looks like. Obedience here is not how you earn the rescue; it is what love looks like after you have been carried out of the house.

Then: *no other gods before me.* The word for other is *acher* (H312), and its root sense is *hinder*, belonging to the back, what comes after. It was not the word for a terrifying rival. It described things that are simply *lesser* — secondary, derivative, trailing.

That reframes the first commandment considerably. God is not warning about impressive competitors. He is saying these are *behind things*. When you chase security or approval from a lesser source, you are not usually doing something dramatically evil. You are facing backwards.

Then the carved image — *pesel* (H6459), from a root meaning to cut, to chisel. A *pesel* is not made by accident. It takes planning: you choose the material, you work it with your hands over time, you return to it.

The danger the second commandment names is almost never a dramatic moment of apostasy. It is the quiet workshop of the heart, patiently fashioning something manageable in place of something untamable. And the fatal problem with the finished product is simple: **you can worship what you made. You cannot be rescued by it.**

And God calls himself *jealous* — *qanna* (H7067), a word used almost exclusively of God, functioning nearly as a title. It is not the petty jealousy of someone who wanted what another got. The image behind it is a husband watching someone try to take his wife: fierce, protective love from someone who knows exactly what he has and refuses to watch it cheapened.

God is not insecure. He is insisting that this bond is real and not a formality, and that he will not pretend otherwise.

### [7-12] Name, Rest, and Weight

*Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain.* The word is *shav* (H7723), and it is richer than emptiness. *Shav* describes something that looks real and is hollow inside — a husk, a counterfeit, a thing that destroys by pretending to be what it is not. It is the same word used elsewhere *for idols*.

Which means the third commandment is closer to the second than we usually read it. To carry God's name as *shav* is to turn his name into an idol: the shape of something true with nothing inside it. This is not primarily about profanity. It is about hollow worship — invoking him as a brand, a warrant, a decoration.

Then the Sabbath, and the operative word is *qadash* (H6942) — *holy*, but the root is *set apart*, pulled out of the ordinary pile and placed somewhere different. Think of everything getting tossed into one drawer, and one small box kept on the counter, reserved.

To keep the day *qadash* means deliberately separating it from the blur, and notice who that is for. God does not need his calendar protected. You need the contrast. Without a set-apart day every day acquires the same texture — relentless, productive, and strangely empty.

And God *blessed* the seventh day — *barak* (H1288), whose root image is to *kneel*. Ancient blessing was physical: you bent low, made contact, and transferred something of yourself. God did not approve the Sabbath from a distance. He knelt over that day and pressed something of himself into it.

So rest is not empty space in the week. It is a day where God has already shown up and left something for you to collect.

Then the turn toward other people, and the first word is one we have been tracking through this whole study. *Honour thy father and thy mother* — *kabed* (H3513). *Heavy.*

Moses's tongue was *kabed*. Pharaoh's heart became *kabed*. God made himself *kabad* at the sea, and the camp saw his *kavod* over the manna. Now the same root lands on your parents: treat them as *substantial*. Give them weight in your decisions, your calendar, your presence.

And notice what the opposite is. Not rudeness. The opposite of *kabed* is treating someone as light — dismissible, easy to set aside.

### [13-17] Five Commands and the Current Underneath Them

The second table is compressed, and each verb is sharper in Hebrew than in English.

*Ratsach* (H7523) — do not murder. Its root means *to dash in pieces*, to shatter beyond repair. It is not clinical language, and it is used almost exclusively for unlawful killing. What gets shattered is never only a body; it is a family, a future, a bearer of God's own image. *Do not shatter what I have made.*

*Na'aph* (H5003) — adultery, and it is an active verb, a deliberate crossing over. Crucially, it is the same word Scripture uses for Israel's unfaithfulness to God. God did not reach for a neutral term. He chose the word that names betrayal of the deepest bond, which is why it cuts as it does: not a rule violated but a covenant fractured.

*Ganab* (H1589) — steal, but not by force. *Ganab* means to move quietly, by stealth, to slip something away while no one is watching. Jacob *ganab*'d away from Laban by night; Rachel *ganab*'d her father's household gods. The thief in this word is not brazen. He is careful.

That is uncomfortably accurate about us. Most of what we take from other people, we take quietly — credit that belongs to someone else, a debt allowed to slip into silence, a truth shaded in our own favor. The command is not aimed only at obvious criminals. It is aimed at the version of us that moves carefully in the dark.

*Shaqer* (H8267) — false witness, and we have met this word before: it is what Pharaoh called God's message. *Shaqer* is something hollow that looks solid, a sham with craft in it. You shaped the story. You knew what you were doing. What this forbids is not getting a fact wrong; it is *building a false structure around someone's name and leaving them to live inside it.*

And then the tenth, which does something the other nine do not. Every previous command targets an action. This one targets a current.

*Chamad* (H2530) — covet. It is not the word for wanting. It means *delighting*, savoring with the eyes and the imagination, rolling something over until you have drunk it in. It is the word used of the fruit that looked *desirable* to Eve.

God is not forbidding the first glance at something good. He is naming what happens when you go back to it, sit down with it, and begin holding it as though it were already yours. Coveting is desire that has pulled up a chair and made itself at home.

And that is the genius of putting it last. You steal because you first coveted. You commit adultery because you first coveted. You lie because you first coveted. Nine commands address the river; the tenth walks upstream and addresses the spring.

### [18-26] The Thick Darkness, and a Dirt Altar

The people see the thunder and the *lightnings* — *lappid* (H3940), which actually means *torches*, flaming brands, the kind a soldier carries into battle or a runner carries through the night.

They were not watching a weather event. The word pictures living, moving flame with purpose and direction — something like an army of fire descending on a mountain. No wonder they ran.

They back away and ask Moses to mediate: *let not God speak with us, lest we die.* And that request is not faithless. It is accurate. They have correctly assessed the situation.

Moses answers with what sounds like a contradiction: *Fear not... God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces.* Do not be afraid, because God has come to make you afraid. Both halves are true, and the word in the middle explains it. *Prove* is *nasah* (H5254), the same verb used when Abraham was asked to offer Isaac. It is not a pop quiz. It is a proving that reveals what is actually inside a person — a forge that burns away pretense.

The thunder was not cruelty. It was a clarifying gift: *this is who I am, so that you know exactly what you are walking with.*

Then one of the most striking sentences in the chapter. *The people stood afar off, and Moses drew near unto the thick darkness where God was.*

The word is *araphel* (H6205), and it is specific: not the soft dark of a bedroom but the dense, impenetrable gloom of a storm system — the sky gone greenish-black, no visibility, the disorienting darkness of being *inside* the weather.

And the text does not say God was above it or behind it. God was *in* it. For Moses to approach God, he had to walk directly into the thing everyone else was running from.

Then, after all that fire, God's instructions about worship are almost jarringly plain: build me an altar of earth. If you use stone, do not cut it with a tool. No steps. And the offering named is *shelamim* (H8002), from *shalom* — wholeness, nothing broken and nothing missing. The *shelamim* was voluntary, brought out of gratitude rather than to settle a debt, and it was the one offering where the worshiper ate part of the meal with God.

A dirt altar and a shared table, right after a mountain on fire. The God who is *araphel* and *lappid* meets ordinary people in ordinary places, and the exchange leaves them more whole than when they came.

## key_insights

1. Grace precedes obligation, structurally. God recites the rescue before he gives a single command, which means the law was never the price of belonging — it is the portrait of what belonging looks like.
2. *Bayit* is a household, not a building. God did not change Israel's address; he extracted them from an entire social system organized around owning them.
3. *Acher* means *behind things*. Idolatry is usually not dramatic rebellion but facing the wrong direction, toward something lesser and derivative.
4. A *pesel* is chiseled, which takes time and intent. The real danger is the patient workshop of the heart fashioning a manageable god — and you can worship what you made, but you cannot be rescued by it.
5. *Shav* is the word for idols, and it is the word in the third commandment. Carrying God's name hollow is a species of idolatry, not merely bad language.
6. God *barak*'d the Sabbath — *knelt over* it and left something in it. Rest is not an empty slot in the week but a day with a deposit already made.
7. *Kabed* — the weight-word that has run through Moses's whole life — is the verb for honoring parents. The opposite of honor is not rudeness; it is treating someone as light.
8. The tenth command reaches upstream of the other nine. *Chamad* is savoring, and it is the current that feeds theft, adultery, and lies alike.
9. God was *in* the *araphel*. Moses approached by walking into the exact darkness the nation was fleeing.

## hard_truths

1. The people were right to be terrified. Moses's *fear not* is immediately followed by *that his fear may be before your faces* — God did not come to be relatable that day.
2. The nation asked for distance and got it. They stood far off while one man went in, and Scripture reports that as what happened, not as a failure God argued them out of.
3. The tenth command makes hiding impossible. Nine can be kept outwardly by anyone disciplined enough; *chamad* is invisible, unenforceable, and the one that indicts everybody.
4. *Ganab* names the quiet version of theft, which means most of us are more implicated by this command than by the dramatic reading of it.
5. God calls himself *qanna* — jealous — and the image is marital, not managerial. A relationship with him is not a formality he will politely tolerate being cheapened.
6. Three months earlier they were slaves with no law. The freedom God gave came with an entire ordering of life attached, and it was given without consultation.

## about_god

He introduces himself by what he has done before he says what he wants. He is *qanna* — fiercely protective of a bond he takes to be real. He kneels over a day to leave something in it, which is a strange and tender image for the God who was throwing torches across a mountain in the same chapter. And he is found inside the *araphel*: not beyond the darkness, not above it, but in it — which means approaching him sometimes requires walking into what everyone else is fleeing.

## about_ourselves

We reverse the order constantly, treating obedience as the entry fee rather than the shape of a life already rescued. We build our idols slowly and call it something else; nobody decides to make a *pesel*, they just keep returning to the workshop. We prefer a mediator to an encounter, and the preference is understandable. And we are all reachable by the tenth commandment, because every one of us knows how to sit down with something that is not ours and let the imagination do its work.

## failures_struggles

Moses does not fail here; he is the one who walks toward the mountain while everyone else backs away. So the struggle belongs to the people, and it is not a sin so much as a limit reaching its end. They had asked for a God who would act, and he did — and when he showed up in *lappid* and thunder, they discovered they could not survive the answer to their own prayer. Their request for Moses to mediate is honest and accurate; the text does not scold them for it. But the picture it leaves is the real struggle of the covenant: a people who genuinely belong to God and cannot stand in his presence, who need someone to go into the darkness on their behalf and come back out. That gap is not resolved in this chapter, and everything that follows in Israel's worship is built around it.

## specific_applications

1. **If obedience feels like earning** — Reread verse 2 before verse 3. Every command in this chapter is addressed to people who were *already* out of Egypt. Put the rescue back in front of the requirement.
2. **If something lesser has your attention** — *Acher* means you are not battling a rival; you are facing backwards. Rather than fighting the lesser thing, turn around.
3. **If you are quietly building something manageable** — Idols are chiseled, not stumbled into. Identify the workshop: what have you been returning to and shaping, week after week, because it is easier to handle than God?
4. **If you use God's name as a warrant** — *Shav* is a husk. Check whether his name is doing real work in your speech or decorating decisions you had already made.
5. **If your parents have become light to you** — *Kabed* asks for weight: presence, time, a place in your actual decisions. The opposite of honor is not hostility but dismissal, and dismissal is easy to do without noticing.
6. **If you keep failing at an action** — Walk upstream to the *chamad*. Ask what you have been savoring in the imagination, because the action is usually downstream of a desire that has already pulled up a chair.
7. **If you are standing at a distance** — There is a real place for reverent distance, and there is a version of it that is avoidance. Moses walked into the *araphel*. Ask which one you are doing.

## reflection_questions

1. Do you live as though obedience earns God's love, or as though it expresses it — and how would someone watching your week be able to tell?
2. What are you facing that is *behind* — lesser, derivative — and how did it get your attention in the first place?
3. What have you been patiently carving? Name the thing you keep returning to because it is more manageable than God.
4. Where do you invoke God's name in a way that is hollow — attached to decisions you had already made?
5. Who in your life has become light to you, and what would giving them weight actually cost this month?
6. What action have you tried repeatedly to stop, without ever addressing the desire you keep rehearsing underneath it?
7. Is there a darkness you have been avoiding that God might actually be inside of?
