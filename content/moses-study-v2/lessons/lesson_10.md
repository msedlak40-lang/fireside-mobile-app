# Lesson 10 — Water from the Rock

**life_stage:** Testing
**key_passage:** Exodus 17:1-16
**character_qualities:** null

> **NEW LESSON.** Added to the study to supply the precedent that makes Lesson 14 ("Striking the Rock," Numbers 20) intelligible: at Rephidim God says *strike* and Moses strikes rightly; at Kadesh God says *speak* and Moses strikes. See the provenance note — the strike/speak pairing is synthesis across two books, not stated by either chapter's enrichment.

## story_narrative

They camped at Rephidim because God told them to, and there was no water there.

That detail is the whole foundation of the chapter and it is easy to read past. They had not wandered off. They had not missed a turn. The text says they journeyed *according to the commandment of the LORD* and pitched camp exactly where they were sent — and the place had nothing to drink.

So the people turn on Moses, and they do not merely complain. They file charges. They demand he account for their thirst as though he were the defendant, and the accusation escalates fast: *Wherefore is this that thou hast brought us up out of Egypt, to kill us and our children and our cattle with thirst?*

Moses's answer cuts to what is actually happening. *Why chide ye with me? Wherefore do ye tempt the LORD?*

And then Moses — the man who parted a sea — screams. *What shall I do unto this people? They be almost ready to stone me.*

God's reply contains no rebuke for either party. Just an instruction, and a promise so striking it is easy to miss: *Go on before the people... and take thy rod, wherewith thou smotest the river, in thine hand, and go. Behold, I will stand before thee there upon the rock in Horeb; and thou shalt smite the rock, and there shall come water out of it.*

**Strike the rock.** God says it plainly. And Moses strikes it, and water comes out, and he does exactly right.

Hold onto that, because forty years later at another place with almost the same name, God will say *speak* to the rock — and Moses will strike it, and it will cost him the promised land.

Then Amalek attacks — a raid on a people barely out of slavery — and the day is decided on a hill rather than in the valley. As long as Moses holds his hands up, Israel prevails. When his arms drop, Amalek prevails. And when he can no longer hold them up, Aaron and Hur sit him on a stone and hold his hands steady, one on each side, until the sun goes down.

And Moses builds an altar and names it: *The LORD is my Banner.*

## verse_sections

### [1-4] They Camped Where God Sent Them, and There Was Nothing to Drink

The chapter opens with a word we met at the Red Sea. They *pitched* — *chanah* (H2583), the concrete tents-in-the-ground word: to tilt downward, to settle, to drive stakes and make camp. Not a rest stop. They unpacked their lives at Rephidim.

And then discovered there was no water.

The Hebrew will not let this be an accident. They *chanah*'d there *by God's command*. **The need and the scarcity arrived together, on purpose.**

There is something painfully recognizable in that sequence. You follow what seemed like clear direction, you settle in — a job, a place, a season — and then you find out the one thing you need is not there. This chapter says plainly that God does that, and it does not apologize for it.

Then the people *chide* — *rib* (H7378), and we will meet this word again at Kadesh. It is not a mild verb. It literally means to grapple or wrestle, and it is the vocabulary of a legal dispute. Picture someone taking you by the collar and demanding you explain yourself. They are not venting frustration; they are filing a charge and naming Moses the defendant.

And Moses sees immediately what they cannot: *why do ye tempt the LORD?* The real complaint was never with him. When you wrestle God's servant, you are wrestling God.

Then they *murmured* — *lun* (H3885), the lodging word from the manna chapter. To stay overnight, to stay past your welcome. Not an outburst but a settled camping-out in grievance. Notice that they *chanah*'d at Rephidim and then *lun*'d in their bitterness. They pitched two camps that day, and only one of them was God's idea.

And Moses *cried* to the LORD — *tsa'aq* (H6817), the shriek. The same word used of Israel under Egyptian slavery, the sound torn out of a person who is cornered.

This is worth saying clearly: Moses is not having a devotional moment. He is screaming, and what he screams is *they are almost ready to stone me*. The man who stood in front of Pharaoh is frightened of his own people.

And God does not rebuke him for it. The shriek is received. Raw, unfiltered desperation aimed *toward* God rather than away from him is itself an act of faith.

### [5-7] Strike the Rock

God's instruction begins with a command that costs something. *Go on before the people* — *avar* (H5674), which carries far more motion than English suggests. It means to cross over, to pass through, to transit decisively from one side to the other; the word used for fording a river.

God does not tell Moses to stand back and manage the crowd. He tells him to *cross through it* — to walk to the front of a mob that is talking about stoning him. That is the position of maximum exposure, and it is the only route to the water. The rod, the elders, the miracle: all of it is on the far side of that step.

Then the promise, and it is the most remarkable sentence in the chapter. *Behold, I will stand before thee there upon the rock in Horeb.*

God stations himself at the rock **before Moses arrives.**

The rock is a *tsur* (H6697) — not a loose stone but a cliff face, a mass of compressed, unyielding rock. The last place on earth you would look for water, and the first place God went.

Whatever in your life looks most like solid, waterless rock is exactly where he tends to plant himself first.

And then: *thou shalt smite the rock.*

**God said strike. Moses struck. Water came. This was obedience, and it was exact.**

This is the hinge of the whole study, so it is worth stating as plainly as possible. At Rephidim, striking the rock *was* the command. Moses raised the same rod he had used on the Nile, brought it down on the cliff face, and did precisely what he was told — and God honored it in front of the whole nation.

Forty years later, at Kadesh, the people will quarrel about water again. The word for their quarrel will be *rib* again. The place will be named Meribah again. Everything about the situation will rhyme. And God's instruction will change by one verb: *speak* to the rock.

And Moses will strike it. Twice.

That is why the method mattered so much the second time. Moses was not improvising at Kadesh — he was repeating. He reached for what God himself had commanded the first time, in a situation that looked identical, and did not notice that the instruction had changed. **Obedience is defined by the current word, not by the precedent, and a method that was faithfulness in one chapter can be disobedience in another.**

The rock at Rephidim is what makes the rock at Kadesh intelligible. It is also what makes it tragic.

And Moses names the place twice over: *Massah*, from *nasah* (H5254), and *Meribah*, from *rib*. *Nasah* carries the specific image of testing a substance to see whether it holds — the root used when a soldier tries on armor to check the fit.

That is what Israel was doing. Treating God as an unproven material and running a stress test to see if he would fail. After the plagues, after the sea, after bread on the ground every morning for weeks, he was still unverified to them.

And the question they asked is the one the place is remembered for: *Is the LORD among us, or not?*

### [8-13] The Hill and the Valley

Then Amalek came and *fought* with Israel — *lacham* (H3898), and the root meaning is startling: *to feed on, to devour, to consume.* It shares a root with *lechem*, bread.

In Hebrew thinking, warfare was not merely clash and maneuver. It was one force trying to *eat* another, the way fire eats wood. Amalek did not come to push Israel back. They came hungry, intending to swallow a people barely free from slavery.

And Moses's response distributes the day across two places. Joshua takes men into the valley. Moses goes up the hill with the rod of God in his hand.

The word for what Joshua is told to do is *natsab* (H5324) — to station yourself, to be posted, planted like a pillar. A soldier taking a fixed position, a sentinel who does not drift. And the word for what Moses does is *alah* (H5927) — to go up, yes, but in the Old Testament it is the word for going up *to worship*, for ascending to God's presence, for offering sacrifice.

There is sacred gravity in it. Moses, Aaron, and Hur did not climb for a better view of the battle. They ascended to do the day's holiest work.

Then the mechanism, stated without embarrassment: *when Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed: and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed.*

The verb is *rum* (H7311) — to rise, to be lifted high, to be *exalted*. It is the same root used when Scripture praises God as exalted. So this is not a description of arm position. **Moses was performing an act of exaltation** — lifting God's authority over the battlefield in real time. When the exaltation dropped, so did Israel.

Your posture in the hidden place shapes what happens in the visible fight. The chapter states that as plainly as Scripture ever states anything.

And then Moses's arms give out, because he is an eighty-year-old man holding a staff over his head through a day-long battle. So Aaron and Hur put a stone under him and sit him down, and stand on either side, and hold his hands up.

*And his hands were steady until the going down of the sun.*

The word for steady is *emunah* (H530) — and in English "steady" sounds mechanical, like a tripod. But *emunah* is the word most often translated **faithfulness**. It describes something so reliable you can put your full weight on it without fear.

His hands were not merely held still. They were held *faithfully*, with the grip you give someone you have committed not to let fall.

What kept Israel alive that day was not Moses's strength. It was two men showing up and not letting go.

And Joshua *discomfited* Amalek — *chalash* (H2522), which does not mean defeated. It means to prostrate, to make something collapse, to drain its strength entirely. Not pushed back. *Emptied out.*

That collapse came from two directions at once: swords swinging in the valley and unseen intercession being held up on the hill, and the text refuses to rank them.

### [14-16] The LORD Is My Banner

God tells Moses to write it in a book, because he intends to *utterly blot out* the remembrance of Amalek. The word is *machah* (H4229) — wiping a surface completely clean, scrubbing a clay tablet smooth so nothing can be read on it.

And we have met this word before, in the mouth of Moses himself. At the golden calf he will say to God: *blot me out of thy book.* Same verb. And David will use it in Psalm 51: *blot out my transgressions.*

The erasure God promises against Israel's enemy is the same erasure he extends as mercy to people who come to him broken. What he does to Amalek, he offers to do to your record.

Then Moses builds an altar and names it *Jehovah-Nissi* — *nes* (H5251), a banner.

The image is specific and military. A *nes* is a pole driven into the high ground of a battlefield with a flag at the top, so that soldiers lost in dust and noise below can look up and find their position.

That is exactly what the raised rod had been all day. Now Moses builds something permanent to say what it meant: **the thing that kept you oriented was not your sword, not your general, and not your own grit. It was him.**

And the last word is *milchamah* (H4421) — war. Not a cold policy position or distant disapproval, but active, hand-on-weapon combat. God is not a judge reviewing a case from a bench. He has personally entered the field, and he will be there *from generation to generation*.

Every time a force rises to crush the weak and derail what God is doing, it will find him already standing there.

## key_insights

1. They camped at Rephidim *by God's command* and found no water. The need and the scarcity arrived together, on purpose — the gap between them is where God shows up, not evidence that you took a wrong turn.
2. God stationed himself at the *tsur* before Moses got there. The hardest, driest, most immovable thing in your life is the first place he plants himself.
3. **God said *strike*, and Moses struck, and it was exact obedience.** This is the precedent that makes Numbers 20 comprehensible: at Kadesh the instruction changed to *speak*, and Moses reached for what had worked before.
4. Obedience is defined by the current word, not by precedent. A method that was faithfulness in one chapter became disobedience in another, and Moses did not notice the verb had changed.
5. *Rum* means to exalt. Moses was not holding up an arm; he was lifting God's authority over a battlefield, and the fight rose and fell with it.
6. *Emunah* — Aaron and Hur held his hands *faithfully*, not just still. What kept Israel alive was two men refusing to let go of someone who could no longer hold himself up.
7. The victory came from two directions at once and Scripture ranks neither. Joshua's visible sword and Moses's hidden hands are given equal weight in the same paragraph.
8. *Nes* is a battlefield pole soldiers look up to find their bearings. Moses built a permanent altar to say that what oriented them was never their own strength.

## hard_truths

1. God led them to a place with no water. This chapter does not present that as a mistake, an oversight, or a test they failed into — it says they went where they were sent.
2. Moses was afraid of the people he rescued. *They be almost ready to stone me* is a shriek, and it came from the man who had faced Pharaoh.
3. *Is the LORD among us, or not?* was asked after the plagues, the sea, and weeks of daily bread. Accumulated evidence does not automatically produce trust.
4. The right method at Rephidim became the wrong one at Kadesh, and the consequence there was permanent. Doing what God told you last time is not the same as obeying him this time.
5. Moses could not hold his own hands up. The greatest intercessor in Israel's history physically ran out, and the day would have been lost without two other men.
6. Amalek attacked people who had just escaped slavery. Freedom did not end the fighting; it changed who was doing it.

## about_god

He goes ahead. The most striking line in the chapter is not the water but *I will stand there before thee upon the rock* — he arrives at the impossible place first. He receives a shriek without correcting it. He distributes a victory across a valley and a hill so that neither can claim it alone. And he enters the field himself: *milchamah*, hand on weapon, from generation to generation, against whatever rises to crush the weak.

## about_ourselves

We read scarcity as evidence of a wrong turn, when it may be the exact address God gave us. We also pitch a second camp without noticing — settling into grievance the way we settle into a place. And we are prone to Moses's specific error in embryo: reaching for the thing that worked last time, in a situation that looks identical, without checking whether the instruction has changed. We are most vulnerable to that precisely when we have a track record.

## failures_struggles

Moses does not fail here — he obeys exactly, including the part that required walking to the front of a crowd discussing whether to stone him. But the struggle in this chapter is real and it runs in both directions.

The people's failure is *nasah*: treating God as unproven material and running a test to see whether he would hold. Their question — *is the LORD among us, or not?* — is not honest doubt after everything they had seen; it is a refusal to trust what they had already witnessed. And they *lun*'d, moving into the grievance rather than bringing it and leaving it.

Moses's own struggle is fear and depletion rather than sin. He shrieks. He tells God the people are ready to kill him. And on the hill his arms simply give out — the plain physical fact that the man everyone was depending on could not sustain the posture the day required. Scripture does not treat either as a failing. It treats them as the true condition of a leader who is carrying more than one body can hold, and it answers them the same way both times: God gives an instruction, and God sends two men to hold him up.

That is worth holding next to Lesson 14. Here Moses is exhausted and obeys. At Kadesh he is exhausted and does not. The difference between those two chapters is not the pressure.

## specific_applications

1. **If you followed clear direction into a place with nothing you need** — Rephidim was God's address. Scarcity where you were sent is not proof you misheard; the gap between the need and the supply is where he has a habit of showing up.
2. **If something in your life looks like solid, waterless rock** — God said *I will stand there before thee* on it. Consider that he is already at the immovable thing, having arrived ahead of you.
3. **If you are about to repeat what worked last time** — This is the lesson's sharpest edge. Check the current instruction against the old one. Moses's greatest failure began as a faithful memory of this exact chapter.
4. **If you are afraid of the people you are serving** — Moses screamed it at God and was not rebuked. Say the true thing about your fear out loud to him rather than managing it.
5. **If you are the one on the hill and no one sees it** — *Alah* is the worship word. The hidden posture and the visible fight were given equal weight here, and the valley did not win without the hill.
6. **If your arms have given out** — Sit on the stone. Moses did. Let someone hold you up; *emunah* means the hands that held his were the faithful thing in the story, not a footnote to it.
7. **If you are the Aaron or the Hur** — What you are doing is not support work. Two men standing on either side of someone who could not hold himself up is what Scripture records as the reason Israel survived that day.

## reflection_questions

1. Where has God led you that turned out to have none of what you needed — and what have you concluded about him from that?
2. What in your life looks most like an immovable rock, and what would change if you believed God was already standing on it?
3. Where are you about to do what worked last time without checking whether the instruction has changed?
4. What are you afraid to say to God plainly, when Moses got away with a shriek?
5. Whose hands are you holding up right now — and if the answer is nobody, who around you has run out of arm?
6. Who could hold yours, and have you let them know you can no longer hold the posture by yourself?
7. What are you looking up at to get your bearings when the dust and noise make you lose your position?
