# Lesson 3 — The Burning Bush

**life_stage:** Calling
**key_passage:** Exodus 3:1-22
**character_qualities:** null

## story_narrative

Moses is eighty years old and keeping sheep that belong to his father-in-law.

That sentence should stop you. Forty years in a palace, forty years in a pasture, and the man who once imagined himself the deliverer of a nation is now an old shepherd working another man's flock in a country that is not his. Whatever he believed about his purpose at forty, he has had four decades to bury it.

And on an ordinary day, driving the flock to the far side of the wilderness, he sees a thornbush on fire.

Fire in that country is not remarkable. What is remarkable is that the bush is still there. It burns and burns and does not char, does not collapse, does not go out. And Moses says the sentence that changes everything: *I will turn aside now, and see this great sight.*

He steps off his route. And only then — only after he turns — does God speak, and the first word out of the fire is his name, said twice. *Moses, Moses.*

What follows is the most consequential job offer in the Old Testament, and it opens with God taking the side of the people Moses had failed to save. *I have seen their affliction. I have heard their cry. I know their sorrows.* Every verb is personal and none of them are distant. Then: *Come now, and I will send you to Pharaoh.*

Moses has exactly one question, and it is the honest one. *Who am I?*

God does not answer it. That is the hinge of the entire chapter. He does not list Moses's qualifications, does not remind him of his Egyptian education or his royal connections or his forty years of desert survival. He changes the subject from Moses to himself: *Certainly I will be with you.*

And when Moses asks what name to carry back to a people who have waited four hundred years, God gives him one that is not a label but a promise: *I AM WHO I AM.* Then he tells Moses exactly how it will go — Pharaoh will refuse, God will act, and Israel will walk out carrying the wealth of the empire that enslaved them.

The bush is still burning. Moses is still standing there with his sandals off. And the forty wasted years turn out to have been the training.

## verse_sections

### [1-6] The Bush That Would Not Burn Up

The chapter's first verb is easy to miss. Moses *led* the flock, and the Hebrew *nahag* (H5090) is not the word for driving animals from behind with a stick. It means to conduct something personally, to go out ahead and bring a reluctant group forward through hard terrain. For forty years Moses had been doing, with sheep, the exact thing God was about to ask him to do with a nation — move a wandering, stubborn crowd through a wilderness toward somewhere better. He thought he was killing time. He was in an apprenticeship, and God did not waste a single ordinary Tuesday of it.

Then the fire. The word for flame is *labbat* (H3827), which describes the sharp, darting tongue of a blaze — not a warm glow but something violent and alive. And the bush is a *seneh* (H5572), a specific word for a scrubby desert bramble: thorned, prickly, useless for anything. The most worthless plant in the landscape.

Put those two words together and you have the theology of the whole chapter in one image. The most consuming thing there is, fully present inside the most fragile and worthless thing there is — and the fragile thing is not destroyed. The fire does everything fire does except the one thing fire always does. If you have been through something that by every reasonable calculation should have burned you to the ground by now, this bush is the picture to hold.

Moses says, *I will turn aside* — *sur* (H5493), a physically weighty word. It means to leave the road you are on. It is used of soldiers breaking ranks and rivers changing course. He did not crane his neck; he abandoned his route. And this is the quiet miracle sitting next to the loud one: not merely that God showed up in a bush, but that Moses could afford to be interrupted. Plenty of divine moments pass unnoticed not because God stopped appearing but because we could not spare the detour.

Watch the sequence carefully: *when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called.* The calling came after the turning. God spoke into the attention Moses chose to give.

And he *called* — *qara* (H7121), which is not shouting across a distance but summoning a particular person by name because you have something specific to say to them. Twice. *Moses, Moses.* In that culture the doubled name signals urgency and intimacy at once: *I know exactly who you are, and this is for you.*

Then: take off your sandals, the ground is *qodesh* (H6944). Not morally scrubbed — *set apart*, belonging to an entirely different category. There was nothing special about that dirt an hour earlier. It became holy the instant God was present in it, which means holiness is not a property of places but a consequence of presence. A patch of nowhere became a sanctuary because the Lord was standing in it.

And Moses covers his face. The verb is *sathar* (H5641), which means concealing something completely, the way you wrap what is precious and fragile. There is reverence in it, not only terror. Moses understood instinctively that looking directly at the real God would undo him. That instinct is worth recovering; we have grown very casual.

### [7-10] I Have Seen, I Have Heard, I Know

God's opening statement is a stack of verbs, and every one of them is intimate.

*I have surely seen the affliction of my people.* *I have heard their cry.* *I know their sorrows.* That last word is *mak'obim* (H4341), and it is not emotional sadness. The root picture is physical pain — the kind that doubles a person over. It is used for wounds and bodily agony. God does not say he is aware of a difficult situation. He says he knows the gut-level, body-felt anguish of people who have been ground down so long they can hardly remember anything else. Not observed from outside. *Known.*

And their *cry* is *tza'aqah* (H6818) — a shriek, the sound torn out of someone under attack or a city under siege. Not a composed prayer. And God says that exact sound *has come unto me*. It did not dissipate on the way up. It arrived.

Then the promise, and the adjective matters. He will bring them to a *good* land — *towb* (H2896), the same word God uses over creation itself. *Towb* is not "adequate" or "survivable." It means beautiful, pleasant, joyful. Paired here with "large," spacious. For people whose entire world was a crowded labor camp and a narrow life, a wide and joyful place would have been nearly unimaginable. God's destination for his people was never merely somewhere they could endure.

And then the turn that must have landed like a blow: *Come now therefore, and I will send thee.* The verb is *shalach* (H7971), and it is not a nudge. *Shalach* is what you do with an arrow leaving a bow or water released from a dam — something loosed with force at a target. It is the same word used when God sends his word, his angels, his prophets. To be *shalach*'d is to carry an authority that is not your own.

Notice what has just happened. God spends four verses establishing that *he* has seen, *he* has heard, *he* knows, and *he* has come down to deliver — and then hands the assignment to a man. The rescue is entirely God's and it runs straight through a human being. Both things are true at once, and the rest of Moses's life lives in that tension.

### [11-15] "Who Am I" Answered With "I AM"

*Who am I, that I should go unto Pharaoh?*

It is the honest question and, at eighty, the reasonable one. And embedded in it is a word that shows the size of what is being asked. *Bring forth* is *yatsa* (H3318) — to break out, to burst into the open, the word used for water bursting from a spring or a child emerging at birth. God is not asking Moses to quietly escort some people through a side door. He is asking him to be the instrument of a forceful, unstoppable emergence.

And here is the thing that should reorganize how we think about calling: **God never disputes Moses's self-assessment.** He does not say *you are more capable than you think.* He does not rehearse the Egyptian education or the royal upbringing. He lets the question stand unanswered and replaces it with a different subject entirely: *Certainly I will be with thee.*

Moses asked a question about Moses. God answered with himself. The gap between the man's smallness and the mission's size was never a problem to be solved — it was the point.

Then God offers a sign, and it is a strange one. The *token* is *'ot* (H226), the same word used for the rainbow after the flood and circumcision as the mark of covenant — a monument planted in time that says *God was here and this was real*. But look where he plants it: *when thou hast brought forth the people, ye shall serve God upon this mountain.* The sign is at the finish line. Moses gets no proof before he goes; he gets a promise that the completed rescue will itself become the monument. Walk toward it, and the evidence builds itself behind you.

So Moses asks for a name. And he is not asking for a label — *shem* (H8034) in that world was character, authority, and track record made audible. A name was a promise compressed into a word. What Moses is really asking is: *what has this God done, and what can four hundred years of suffering people count on him to do?*

The answer is *ehyeh asher ehyeh* — I AM WHO I AM, built on *hayah* (H1961). And the crucial thing is that it is active, not static. It does not mean *I exist the way a rock exists.* It carries becoming, coming-to-pass, presence inside an unfolding situation: *I will be, actively, in whatever you are walking into.*

Which is to say: God gives Moses the burning bush as a name. A presence that burns without burning out.

And he adds *zeker* (H2143) — my *memorial* to all generations. Not a mental note; in the ancient world a *zeker* was a marker carved in stone so that travelers generations later would know what happened here. God is saying: let my track record with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob be the standing monument. When you cannot feel me, the stone is still there. I have kept my word before.

### [16-22] Told the Ending Before the Beginning

Before Moses takes a single step, God briefs him on the whole campaign — including the part that will go badly.

First, what Moses is to tell the elders: *I have surely visited you*. The verb is *paqad* (H6485), and it is not a friendly check-in. Picture a commanding officer arriving unannounced to inspect his troops personally — boots on the ground, eyes on every detail, official attention that leads directly to action. To a people who had been invisible to power for four centuries, *I have paqad'd you* would have been staggering. It means: I have shown up, I have assessed it, and I am intervening.

And what he is lifting them out of is *oni* (H6040) — affliction, but with the specific image of being pressed down, crushed low, forced into submission against your will. God names the crushing precisely rather than speaking in generalities about hardship. Then he says he will *bring them up*, a verb of ascent — pulling someone up out of a pit by the hand. Not relocation. Elevation.

Then the warning, and it is a mercy. *I am sure that the king of Egypt will not let you go.* The Hebrew is *yada* (H3045) — knowing by direct sight, the certainty of someone who has seen this a hundred times. God is not predicting; he is reporting. He has looked at Pharaoh's heart the way you look at a stone wall.

Think about what it costs God to say this in advance, and what it saves Moses. The resistance is coming, it will be total, and it is not evidence that Moses misheard the call. Most of us interpret opposition as a sign we got the assignment wrong. God removes that interpretation before Moses ever leaves Midian.

And then what God will do about it: *wonders*, *nifleotai* (H6381), from a root meaning to be separated out — so far beyond ordinary that they occupy a category of their own. Not impressive coincidences. Events that cannot be filed under any natural explanation, that force even a stubborn observer to concede a power operating outside his categories.

The chapter ends with the strangest promise of all. Israel will not leave empty-handed; God will give them *chen* (H2580) in the eyes of the Egyptians — a word somewhere between graciousness and warm goodwill, the feeling that makes a person want to open their hands toward you. It is never earned; it lands on you. And the women will *ask* — *sha'al* (H7592), the same verb used when Hannah poured out her heart before God. Not sneak, not steal. Ask, face to face, and be met with open hands.

God does not redeem his people by having them crawl out the back door in the dark. He gives them the standing to make a request of their former owners, and arranges the answer before they finish asking.

## key_insights

1. God spoke only after Moses turned aside. The calling did not interrupt him against his will — it came into the attention he chose to give, which means our capacity to be interrupted is not a small spiritual matter.
2. The *seneh* is the theology in miniature: consuming holiness fully present inside a worthless thorn shrub, and the shrub survives. God's presence does not destroy what it fills.
3. God never answered "Who am I?" He answered with "I will be with you," and refused throughout to discuss the gap between Moses and the mission. The mission never rested on Moses being enough.
4. *Ehyeh* is an active verb, not a static one — not "I exist" but "I will be present and at work in whatever you walk into." God's name is the burning bush translated into language.
5. God planted the confirming sign at the *finish line*, not the start. Moses had to obey first and receive the proof on the other side of it.
6. Forty years of *nahag* — leading a flock through wilderness — was the exact skill the assignment required. The wasted decades were the training, and Moses could not have known that at any point during them.

## hard_truths

1. God told Moses in advance that Pharaoh would refuse. Obedience does not buy cooperation, and resistance is not evidence you misheard.
2. God did not argue Moses out of his sense of inadequacy, and he may not argue you out of yours either. He simply declines to make it the deciding factor.
3. The sign comes after the obedience. If you are waiting for confirmation before you move, you are waiting for something God explicitly did not offer here.
4. It took eighty years and forty of them in obscurity before the call came. God's preparation timelines are not negotiable and are frequently indistinguishable, from inside, from being forgotten.
5. God said *I have seen, I have heard, I know* — and the people had already been crying out for generations before he said it. His knowing is real and it does not mean immediate.
6. Moses had to go back to the exact country he had fled in disgrace. The calling led directly into the place of his greatest failure, not around it.

## about_god

He sees, hears, and *knows* — and the word for knows is the language of bodily pain, not detached awareness. He does not observe suffering from a distance; he knows it from inside. He attaches his name to dead men's promises and calls that his memorial, which means his character is verifiable rather than merely asserted. He is *ehyeh*: not a static existence but an active presence, continually becoming what the moment requires. And he is a God who prepares his servants for the hard part in advance, telling Moses about Pharaoh's refusal before it could break him.

## about_ourselves

We measure calling by capacity, and God does not. Every objection Moses raises is a calculation about himself, and every answer God gives is about God — which suggests that most of our self-assessment, even when accurate, is simply beside the point. We also want the sign before the step, and we are rarely given it. And we read our obscure seasons as forfeiture, not formation; Moses spent forty years leading sheep and could not have told you it mattered.

## failures_struggles

The struggle here is Moses's own, and it is not humility — it is a self-assessment that has hardened into an argument. *Who am I?* is honest at first, and by the end of the next chapter it will have become a refusal. Forty years of exile did real damage: the man who once acted too quickly now will not act at all. He asks for a name, for proof, for credentials, for anything that will let him keep evaluating the assignment instead of accepting it. And underneath is something the text does not say out loud but the geography does — God is sending him back to Egypt, to the scene of a murder, to a people who once said *who made you a prince over us*. Moses is being asked to walk directly back into the wreckage of the last time he tried.

## specific_applications

1. **If you are too busy to be interrupted** — The call came *after* Moses turned aside. Build enough margin that a burning bush could actually get your attention; God spoke into the attention Moses chose to give.
2. **If you feel unqualified for what is in front of you** — Stop waiting for God to talk you into feeling capable. He never did it for Moses. The relevant question is not whether you are enough but whether he is with you.
3. **If you are waiting for confirmation before you obey** — The *'ot* was planted at the finish line. Some signs are only visible from the far side of the step, and refusing to move until you see one guarantees you never will.
4. **If you are facing resistance and taking it as a sign you were wrong** — God told Moses the refusal was coming precisely so it would not break him. Opposition was in the brief from the beginning.
5. **If you are in a season that looks wasted** — Moses's forty years of *nahag* were the exact curriculum. Ask not whether this season is going anywhere, but what it is putting in your hands.
6. **If you are being sent back to a place you failed** — Moses's assignment was Egypt. God is not always in the business of routing us around the wreckage; sometimes the calling goes straight through it.

## reflection_questions

1. What have you stopped turning aside for, and what might you be walking past because your route cannot spare the detour?
2. Where are you still asking "Who am I?" long after God has answered "I will be with you" — and what would change if you accepted that he is not going to address the first question?
3. What sign are you waiting for before you obey, and is it one God has actually promised you?
4. Where in your life has fire been burning a long time without consuming you, and have you ever named that as God's presence rather than your own endurance?
5. What forty-year stretch of your life do you consider wasted, and what skill or instinct did it quietly put in your hands?
6. Is there a place, a person, or a failure you have decided you can never go back to — and is that exactly where you are being sent?
7. What would it mean to treat your ordinary Wednesday as *qodesh* — not because of anything about the day, but because of who is present in it?

## next_lesson_preview

God has answered every objection, and Moses has one more. Then another. Then another, until the excuses run out and what is left underneath them is simply *send somebody else*. What God does with that — the signs, the anger, and the brother already on his way — sets the pattern for how God handles a called person who does not want to go.
